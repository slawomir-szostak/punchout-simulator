What problem PunchOut solves

A large organization buys from many suppliers. It wants every purchase to flow through one procurement platform (SAP Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, Oracle, Workday …) so that spend is controlled, approvals are enforced, and orders are auditable. But it does not want to maintain a copy of every supplier's catalog — prices, availability, and configurable products change constantly and are best known by the supplier.

PunchOut is the standard that resolves this tension. Instead of importing a catalog, the buyer's procurement platform lets an approved user "punch out" to the supplier's own live web catalog, shop there, and bring a finished cart back into the procurement platform for approval and ordering. The supplier keeps control of the catalog experience; the buyer keeps control of the process and the money.

Note

PunchOut is sometimes called "round-trip" or "catalog interchange". The dominant protocol is cXML, created by Ariba in 1999 and now spoken by most major procurement platforms. A separate older protocol, OCI, is covered briefly later for contrast.

The actors

Actor Role in the process
Buyer user (requisitioner) An employee who needs to buy something. Works entirely inside the procurement platform's UI.
Procurement platform The buyer's system of record (Ariba, Coupa, …). Initiates PunchOut, enforces approvals, issues the purchase order. Also called the buyer system.
Supplier catalog The supplier's web storefront, adapted to receive PunchOut sessions and return carts as structured documents.
Approver / finance Downstream roles inside the procurement platform that approve the requisition and reconcile the invoice.

In cXML terms the procurement platform is the buyer and the supplier's catalog is the supplier. Every message carries credentials identifying both parties and a shared secret proving the pair is authorized.

The PunchOut round-trip

A complete PunchOut purchase is a round-trip: the user leaves the procurement platform, shops on the supplier site, and returns with a cart — then, later, the platform sends the actual order. There are four distinct exchanges, and they alternate between server-to-server calls and browser-mediated posts.

Fig. 1: The four exchanges of a cXML PunchOut round-trip.

Step by step

  1. Session setup. When the user clicks "punch out to Supplier X", the procurement platform makes a server-to-server PunchOutSetupRequest. It carries the credentials, a shared secret, the BrowserFormPost URL where the cart should be returned, and flags like operation and deploymentMode. The supplier replies with a PunchOutSetupResponse containing a one-time StartPage URL.
  2. Shopping. The user's browser is redirected to the StartPage and shops on the supplier's site like any web store — searching, configuring, adding to cart. The procurement platform is not involved during this step.
  3. Cart return. When the user clicks "check out" or "transfer cart", the supplier does not place an order. Instead it returns the cart as a structured PunchOutOrderMessage, posted back through the browser to the BrowserFormPost URL the platform supplied in step 1.
  4. Order. The cart lands in the procurement platform as a draft requisition. It goes through approval. Only then — possibly days later — does the platform send the real OrderRequest (the purchase order) server-to-server to the supplier, which acknowledges with an OrderResponse.
Important

The cart return is not an order. It is a proposal that re-enters the buyer's controlled process. The legally and financially meaningful document is the later OrderRequest. Confusing the two is the single most common conceptual mistake in PunchOut integration.

Operations: create, edit, inspect

The PunchOutSetupRequest carries an operation attribute that tells the supplier why the user is punching out:

Operation Business meaning
create A fresh shopping trip. The user starts with an empty cart.
edit The user already has items from this supplier in their requisition and wants to change them. The platform sends the existing line items so the supplier can pre-load the cart; the user adjusts and returns the revised cart.
inspect The user wants to view a previously selected item on the supplier site (e.g. to re-check specs or price) without necessarily changing it.

For edit and inspect, the procurement platform includes the existing line items (as ItemOut blocks) inside the PunchOutSetupRequest. A business-complete supplier honours those by restoring the cart to that state.

Tip

The simulator supports this on both ends. The outbound document fully reflects the operation — edit/inspect carry operation="edit" and the prior cart items as ItemOut. The Mode-B mock supplier also honours an incoming edit by pre-loading the catalog (quantities pre-filled, non-catalog items shown as extra rows) and an inspect by showing the item read-only. See the Operations & usage guide for the details.

Where the user starts: store, aisle or product

A user does not always enter a supplier's site through its front door. The buyer's own catalog can list entries published by the supplier — one for the whole store, one per product group, or one per product — and each entry is a doorway into the supplier's site. The standard calls this the granularity of a PunchOut item:

Level What the buyer's catalog lists What the user does at the supplier
Store One entry for everything the supplier sells. Searches the supplier's site for what they need.
Aisle Several entries, each for a group of related products. Lands in the right section and picks from it.
Product One entry per product or service. Lands on that product; no searching.

When the user clicks an entry, the PunchOutSetupRequest names it in a SelectedItem, and the supplier decides what to show. Without one, the supplier presents its whole store. This is often called Level 2 PunchOut.

The business trade-off is the supplier's: the better its own search and configuration tools, the broader the entries can be; a supplier with a thin site serves users better with product-level entries. For the buyer the benefit is that users search in one familiar place — their own catalog — and still get live prices and availability from the supplier.

Tip

In the simulator, a session can be started from a catalog item, to check that a real supplier lands the user on that item rather than on its home page. The mock supplier honours a SelectedItem as well, opening on the product, on the group it matches, or on the whole store with a note when it does not know the item.

How a session ends

Clicking "transfer cart" is one way out of the supplier's site, not the only one, and what comes back does not always mean "add these items".

  • A cart with items replaces what the session had.
  • A cancelled session. A supplier can offer a Cancel button; it returns an empty cart, telling the buyer's system that nothing is coming and the user is back.
  • An empty cart after an edit means the opposite of nothing: the items the user went in to change are to be removed from the requisition. If the user simply backed out, the supplier says so explicitly (a "no change" status) and the requisition is left as it was.
  • After an inspect, whatever comes back is ignored. The user was only looking.

A supplier also states, with every cart, what the buyer may do with it later: nothing more than order it, reopen it to look, or reopen it to change it. A buyer system is expected to respect that — not to offer "edit" on a cart the supplier marked as final.

Important

These cases are where integrations quietly go wrong: a buyer system that treats a cancelled session as an error, or one that keeps items a user deleted on the supplier's site. Each is easy to produce in the simulator and worth testing before go-live.

The money: totals, tax, shipping and who pays

A cart and an order both carry a total, and the standard is specific about what it is: the cost of the items, excluding any tax and shipping. Tax and shipping are stated beside it, never folded in. A total that silently includes them is one of the most common integration mistakes, because the two systems then disagree by exactly the amount nobody thought to check.

Who supplies those figures is a business decision, and it differs from customer to customer:

  • Tax may be computed by the buying organization — it knows its own tax position — or quoted by the supplier with the cart. Some buyers want tax in the returned cart; others insist on none and add their own.
  • Shipping is typically quoted by the supplier, since it knows what delivery costs, and carried into the order.
  • Cost allocation. A buyer may attach accounting to each order line — cost centre, general-ledger account, project — saying who inside the organization pays. The supplier does not act on it, but returns it on the invoice so the buyer can reconcile.

Because these are agreed per customer rather than fixed by the standard, suppliers configure them customer by customer in practice.

Tip

The simulator mirrors that. A buyer and a supplier each have defaults for tax and shipping, and every buyer–supplier connection can override them — switch them off, or set figures specific to that pair. The validator checks the arithmetic and names a total that includes charges.

After the order: changes and cancellations

A purchase order is not always final. Quantities change, a line is dropped, a project is cancelled. cXML handles all of this with the same document that placed the order:

  • a change order is an OrderRequest marked as an update,
  • a cancellation is one marked as a delete.

Both keep the original order number, carry a rising version, and name the document they follow, so a supplier can always reconstruct the history of an order from the first version to the last.

Fig. 2: An order's life — the original, any number of changes, and possibly a cancellation, each referring to the one before.

Suppliers differ in what they accept: some take change orders automatically, some only before shipping, some not at all. A supplier can advertise this (see the next section), and it is worth establishing during onboarding rather than with the first real change.

Finding out what a supplier supports

Before any of this, one system can simply ask the other what it can do. The Profile transaction is a standard question — "which requests do you accept, and where do I send them?" — and the answer lists them: PunchOut setup, orders, whether attachments and change orders are accepted, and the address for each.

Where buyer and supplier are connected through a network hub, the hub asks suppliers this periodically and keeps the answers, so buyers rarely see it. In a direct connection it is used less — the addresses are usually exchanged by hand at onboarding — and not every supplier answers it. Where it is supported, it is the quickest check that an endpoint is alive, speaks cXML and accepts your credentials.

cXML versus OCI

"Supporting PunchOut" is not a single target. The protocol family matters:

Fig. 3: cXML returns a structured XML document; OCI returns flat form fields. They are different protocols, not versions of one.

  • cXML returns a structured XML document and is the dominant family (Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, Oracle, Workday).
  • OCI (Open Catalog Interface, SAP SRM) returns flat NEW_ITEM-* form fields to a HOOK_URL. It is a fundamentally different protocol.

Even within cXML, platforms vary in credential style, the deploymentMode flag, how the cart is posted back (cxml-urlencoded / cxml-base64 / raw), how OrderRequest attachments are MIME-encoded, and which extrinsic fields they attach. A deeper treatment of these per-platform differences lives in the companion analysis "How Procurement Platforms Differ in PunchOut Requests".

Note

punchout-simulator speaks cXML only. OCI is described here for context so that integrators understand why a given supplier or platform may not be a match for the tool.

Where punchout-simulator fits

The hard part of PunchOut integration is that both ends are live systems and each blames the other when something fails. The simulator gives you one honest, fully inspectable end so you can isolate the real system under test.

Fig. 4: Mode A tests a real supplier by simulating the buyer; Mode B tests a real procurement platform by simulating the supplier.

Two modes, two questions

  • Mode A — "is the supplier integration correct?" The simulator acts as a virtual procurement platform. You compose and send the buyer-side documents to a real supplier endpoint and inspect exactly what comes back. Use this when you are a buyer onboarding a supplier, or a supplier validating your own catalog against a clean, standard buyer.
  • Mode B — "is the buyer integration correct?" The simulator acts as a virtual supplier serving a mock catalog. A real procurement platform punches out to it, the user shops, and the cart is posted back. Use this when you are a supplier testing how a specific procurement platform talks to you, or a buyer validating your platform configuration against a clean, standard supplier.

What the simulator gives you

  • A clean reference counterparty. One end behaves to spec, so any deviation is attributable to the real system.
  • Full message visibility. Every PunchOutSetupRequest, PunchOutSetupResponse, PunchOutOrderMessage, OrderRequest and OrderResponse is captured per session and viewable as raw cXML.
  • Validate-before-send. Outbound documents can be checked against expectations (credentials, currency, address completeness, operation/item coherence) before they leave the tool. The rules follow the published cXML standard, and say plainly what a problem means for the business flow.
  • The whole life of a purchase, not just the happy path. Punching out from a catalog item; a cancelled session; an emptied cart; tax, shipping and cost allocation, per customer; change orders and cancellations; asking a supplier what it supports.
  • A mock catalog that behaves like a shop, so a test walks through the session the way a buyer user would.
  • Profiles and product lists. Platform-specific quirks (cXML version, address mode, extrinsics) are captured as reusable buyer profiles; mock catalogs are reusable product lists shared across suppliers.

A worked business scenario

A distributor is onboarding with a customer who runs Coupa. The two integration questions can be tackled independently:

  1. Supplier proves its catalog (Mode A). The distributor points the simulator's virtual buyer at its own catalog endpoint, using a Coupa-shaped profile. It punches out, shops, returns a cart, and sends an order — confirming its catalog produces a well-formed PunchOutOrderMessage and accepts an OrderRequest, before the customer's Coupa ever touches it.
  2. Buyer proves its platform (Mode B). The customer points its Coupa instance at the simulator's virtual supplier. A buyer user punches out to the mock catalog, shops the sample assortment, and returns a cart into Coupa — confirming the platform's connection, credentials, and cart handling against a known-good supplier.

With the basics passing, each side can go on to the cases that usually surface only in production:

  1. The awkward endings. The distributor cancels a session, empties a cart in an edit, and returns a cart marked as not editable — and the customer checks that Coupa does the right thing with each.
  2. The money. The connection for this customer is set to return carts without tax, because this customer computes its own; the order that comes back carries the customer's tax and cost centre, and the totals agree.
  3. A change of mind. The customer changes a quantity on an approved order and later cancels it; the distributor confirms its system follows the order through all three documents.

When both halves pass against the simulator, the only remaining variable is the real-to-real handshake — and both sides already know their own end is correct.
